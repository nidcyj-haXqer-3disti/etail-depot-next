# Merchant dashboard and scoped roles

Research refreshed **2026-09-09**. This supports the user's latest direction: two working services, Cardholder and Merchant, with their own underlying authority and hierarchy. Earlier research that organized the interface around Personal, Household and Seller is historical context, not the target for this redesign.

The useful Amex reference is a **merchant work dashboard with a persistent scope**, followed by payment and dispute drilldowns. A directory can help switch context; it should not replace the service dashboard. This is an Etail design conclusion from the references below, not a claim about Amex's private implementation.

## Dashboard and task flows to build

Everything in this section is an **Etail proposal** applying the user's chosen model. The source IDs identify the inspiration, not an Amex requirement or a claim that the proposed feature is already implemented.

| Surface | Proposed behavior | Scope and authority |
|---|---|---|
| Merchant Overview | Open directly to collected payments, refunds, available funds, transfers, channel readiness and work needing attention. Each number links to its filtered records. Keep the selected primary merchant, included submerchants, period and currency visible. A merchant switcher is a compact control, not the content of the page. Inspiration: MD01–MD04. | Primary administrators can inspect their authorized network. A delegated submerchant user sees only its own totals and queue. An empty service gives a meaningful creation or invitation next step, without exposing account-container choices. |
| Payments | Start with a useful period and filter; expose amount, state, date, reference and the actual collecting merchant. Row detail connects original payment, refund and transfer records without losing scope or filters. Separate gross collection from available funds and money transferred. Inspiration: MD02–MD03. | Never use a visible row count or available filter option to reveal a sibling outside the viewer's scope. Network aggregation requires primary authority; selecting a child does not promote the actor or broaden actions. |
| Payment channels | Open one saved channel, inspect its terms and available presentation formats, review activation, then use its sandbox preview. All formats refer to the same payment obligation. Put payment history and any remaining action beside the channel. | Actual grants decide create/edit/activate/close access. Sharing a channel or allowing collection is not permission to manage people, refund, transfer funds or access Cardholder data. |
| Disputes | Show the urgent work queue, then case detail, timeline, response preparation, review and durable confirmation. Display who must act next. Returning restores the queue context. Inspiration: MD04–MD05. | Viewing a case and answering it are separate permissions. Do not simulate a provider decision or infer a deadline when the application has none. If the current sandbox has only local review, state that at the consequential action. |
| Submerchants | Within a primary merchant, show named child units, current access, readiness and their useful activity. Adding a child is an explicit merchant task. Use names and relationship position rather than historical household/personal types. Inspiration: MD06–MD08. | The user's delegated collection-unit model supplies the relationship semantics. A person may administer one primary while only helping another child; do not assign one global primary/sub identity to that person. |
| People and access | Show person, exact merchant scope, allowed tasks, invitation state and access history. Review the named scope and permissions before granting. Make revocation and its effects understandable. Inspiration: MD08–MD09. | Roles are helpful presets, not an alternative authority engine. Merchant access and Cardholder access have independent grants and revocation. No credential sharing or implicit employee access through unrelated membership. |
| Payment setup and notifications | Put gateway/enrollment readiness and blocked next steps beside the merchant they affect. Keep alerts linked to durable work records. Notification preferences do not establish financial permission. Inspiration: MD07–MD08. | Test payment readiness is distinct from live processing approval, business verification and payout eligibility. Do not show a bank payment, fee or external message as completed without that actual outcome. |

For narrow screens, preserve identity, amount, status, next action and the selected scope before optional table columns. A row can open a focused detail panel; filters must remain labelled, keyboard operable and recoverable after navigation. This is an Etail usability requirement, not a claim about the historical Amex mobile layout.

## Hierarchy model: the useful comparison and its limit

Amex's published primary/dependent MID display and CAP access rules establish that an enterprise merchant experience can combine hierarchical reporting with separately assigned work. They do **not** prove that Etail's submerchant is an Amex dependent location, that a portal administrator is a payment facilitator, or that choosing a parent allocates acquiring liability. MD06 and MD08 support that narrower comparison.

Etail's user has separately chosen a responsible primary merchant with delegated collection units, multiple named submerchants and primary oversight. Preserve that as the product model: Merchant service → responsible primary merchant → named submerchant; people receive authority over the exact service and scope. Cardholder relationships form a separate hierarchy. Internal legal/applicant or legacy account references may preserve provenance, but they must not remain mandatory product choices, feature tiers or inherited authorization routes.

The next design should therefore make the Merchant service itself useful on first entry, maintain one coherent merchant hierarchy beneath it, and keep its commands grounded in that hierarchy. A changed landing page alone would not satisfy this correction. This research does not assess release readiness, a percentage of Amex parity, or the private architecture of American Express.
